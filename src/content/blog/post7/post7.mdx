Stereo camera pairs recover depth from disparity (the pixel shift between matching points in two images) using the pinhole camera model. This post derives the core geometric relationships: minimum sensing distance, dead-band width, depth resolution, and 3D reconstruction from disparity. Interactive calculators are included throughout to explore how these quantities change with baseline, field of view, and resolution.

## Stereo Camera Geometry

### Variables

| Symbol | Name | Units | Description |
|--------|------|-------|-------------|
| $b$ | Baseline | mm | Distance between the two camera optical centers |
| $HFOV$ | Horizontal field of view | degrees | Field of view of each camera, measured horizontally |
| $W$ | Horizontal resolution | pixels | Horizontal resolution of the camera |
| $\Omega$ | Focal length | pixels | Focal length of the camera |
| $Z$ | Depth | mm | Distance from the camera plane to an object |
| $d$ | Disparity | pixels | Horizontal pixel shift between the left and right image for a given point |
| $\delta d$ | Disparity increment | pixels | Smallest disparity increment (1 for integer disparity, less for sub-pixel methods) |
| $D_v$ | Minimum stereo depth distance | mm | Minimum distance where both cameras see the same object (stereo overlap begins) |
| $F$ | Scene width | mm | Real-world width of the scene captured by the camera at distance $Z$ |
| $B$ | Dead-band width | pixels | Width of the left/right dead band where depth cannot be computed |
| $\text{res}_Z$ | Depth range resolution | mm | Smallest resolvable depth difference at distance $Z$ |
| $u_L$, $u_R$ | Horizontal image coordinate | pixels | Horizontal pixel coordinate of a point in the left/right image, relative to the principal point |
| $v_L$ | Vertical image coordinate | pixels | Vertical pixel coordinate of a point in the left image, relative to the principal point |
| $X$, $Y$, $Z$ | 3D coordinates | mm | Coordinates of a reconstructed point in the left camera's coordinate system |
| $R$ | Mutual distance | mm | Real-world distance between two targets at the same depth $Z$ |
| $r$ | Mutual pixel distance | pixels | Distance between the two targets' projections |


### Minimum Stereo Depth Distance $D_v$

Objects closer than $D_v$ fall outside the overlapping field of view of both cameras, so disparity cannot be computed there.

$$
D_v = \frac{b}{2} \cdot \tan\!\left(90° - \frac{HFOV}{2}\right)
$$

### Scene Width $F$ at Distance $Z$

The real-world width $F$ of the scene captured by the camera at distance $Z$:

$$
F = 2 \cdot Z \cdot \tan\!\left(\frac{HFOV}{2}\right)
$$

### Dead-Band Width $B$

The stereo dead band $B$ is the number of pixels on each side of the image where no depth information is available. 
Substituting $F$ into $B = 2 D_v \tan\!\left(\frac{HFOV}{2}\right) \cdot \frac{W}{F}$ and simplifying:

$$
B = \frac{W \cdot D_v}{Z} \quad \text{(pixels)}
$$

### Depth Range Resolution $\text{res}_Z$

The stereo depth estimate for a point at distance $Z$ is derived from its disparity. Because disparity is quantized, two points at slightly different depths may produce the same disparity value and be indistinguishable. The smallest depth difference that can be resolved is:

$$
\text{res}_Z = \frac{Z^2}{\Omega \cdot b} \cdot \delta d
$$

where all lengths are in mm. Resolution degrades quadratically with distance: doubling $Z$ makes depth four times coarser.

### 3D Reconstruction from Disparity

All three coordinates ($Z$, $X$, $Y$) are expressed in the left camera coordinate system, with the left camera's optical center as the origin.
A stereo matcher finds the horizontal pixel shift $d = u_L - u_R$ (disparity) between corresponding points in the left and right images.

#### Depth

$$
Z = \frac{\Omega \cdot b}{d}
$$

#### Horizontal Position

$$
X = u_L \frac{Z}{\Omega} = u_L \frac{b}{d}
$$

#### Vertical Position

$$
Y = v_L \frac{Z}{\Omega} = v_L \frac{b}{d}
$$

#### Distance Between Two Points (2D)

This relationship holds for two targets lying on the same depth plane $Z$: $R$ is the distance between them within that plane. The distance between two targets $R$ and the distance between their projections $r$ satisfy the following equation:

$$
R = r \frac{Z}{\Omega}
$$

Once a target's position (or the distance between two targets) is reconstructed this way, its accuracy depends on how well the pinhole model's inputs (depth, focal length, pixel localization) are known.
